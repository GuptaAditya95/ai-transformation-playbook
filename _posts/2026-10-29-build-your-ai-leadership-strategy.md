Most organisations don't lack AI ideas. They lack a way to tell which ideas are real bets and which are
experiments wearing a bet's clothes. This lesson is about building an AI portfolio that's aligned to the
business's North Star.

## Key pointers

- **Strategy starts from the business, not from AI.** Take the core business strategy and treat AI as fuel
  for it, measured by the KPIs that already define success. Start from a real goal or bottleneck, then ask
  whether AI helps.
- **A bet is not an experiment.** A genuine bet is tied to a value driver (revenue, margin, risk or customer
  lifetime value) and to a North Star KPI. Tool-driven ideas ("we should try X") are experiments.
- **Strategy, framework and roadmap are three different things.** They get blurred together, which is why
  so many AI plans feel busy but directionless.
- **Go deep and narrow.** Concentrate on two or three high-priority bets rather than spreading thin.
- **Most AI value lives in people, process and culture, not in models.** The model is the easy part. The
  real work is changing how the workflow and the people in it operate.

### The three objects

| Object | Answers | What it contains |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | Why and where | Which goals AI serves, and which bets matter most |
| **Framework** | How | The method for choosing and testing bets |
| **Roadmap** | When | The chosen work, sequenced over time |

## The framework: The Translation Method

The Translation Method turns business goals into a small, measurable AI portfolio. It runs in seven steps:

| Step | What you do | Question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Pull goals** | Start from business objectives that have already been committed: revenue, cost, cycle time. | What has leadership already signed up to deliver? |
| 2. **Attach KPIs** | Give each objective a primary KPI, a guardrail metric that must not get worse while you chase the KPI, and an accountable executive sponsor. | What could we break by optimising this? Who owns the outcome? |
| 3. **Map the workflow** | Find the workflow or decision point where the metric is actually won or lost. That's often not the department that owns the metric. | Where does the result really change? |
| 4. **Ask AI** | Only now assess AI candidates against that point in the workflow. | Which business metric will improve, and by how much? |
| 5. **Pick 2–3 bets** | Concentrate on the few candidates with the clearest link to a North Star KPI. | If we could only fund three, which would they be? |
| 6. **Park the experiments** | Anything tool-driven or not tied to a North Star KPI is an experiment. Label it and keep it separate. | Is this a bet or a pilot looking for a problem? |
| 7. **Set a baseline and Go/No-Go gate** | Measure today's number and agree the threshold for continuing *before* building anything. | What result would make us stop? |

The method translates strategy into three answers: **where AI matters most, when to invest, and how
you'll know it works.**

## Putting it to work

Run every proposed AI initiative through three filter questions:

1. **Which goal does this serve?** Every metric should connect to a committed objective.
2. **What KPI would move?** If no metric shifts, the initiative lacks strategic grounding.
3. **Is this strategy or a standalone experiment?** Separate aligned bets from disconnected pilots, and
   report them separately.

A practical way to start: list your current AI initiatives, run them through the three questions, and sort
them into *bets* and *experiments*. Most teams find they have many experiments and few real bets.

**Watch out for:**
- Starting with a tool or model and hunting for a use case.
- Chasing a KPI without a guardrail, so another metric quietly gets worse.
- Mapping to the owning department instead of the decision point where the metric moves.
- Building before the baseline and Go/No-Go gate are agreed.
- Treating the model as the value, when most of it comes from changes to people, process and culture.
